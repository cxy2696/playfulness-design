01 · Concept A story machine with gears

Design Concept

Transforming a user's stories with random elements and antique-inspired twists (e.g., imagining memories as clockwork adventures). It encourages voluntary involvement through “what if” scenarios, drawing from medieval automata for added wonder.

Key features (conceptual)

Story

Personalised tales

Generates personalised stories (e.g., MemoryLane), with antique motifs for surprise.

Emotion

Reads the room

Adapts via emotions (e.g., facial recognition like LONG-REMI) to maintain positive engagement.

Play

Small games

Incorporates gamified elements like proverb completions or rhythm games, evoking physical and emotional responses.

Surprise

Controlled randomness

Uses controlled randomness for surprises, fostering curiosity, now inspired by historical mechanisms.

03 · Connections Play principles & automata

Connections to Antique Automata

The module mentions medieval robots (500–1500 CE) as precursors. These automata embodied play through hidden mechanisms, fostering curiosity and ambiguity, much like modern designs. Here is a table comparing concepts.

Seven playful principles mapped to historical automata.
Module concept Description from module Related antique example Connection to playfulness
Affective + abstract robotic motion As in Pinokio Lamp (2012) and Apple's prototype tabletop robot (2025), subtle movements enhance acceptance. The Mechanical Monk (1560) by Juanelo Turriano: clockwork friar walks, prays, moves eyes and lips. Evokes affection through lifelike gestures, creating emotional engagement like modern AI motion.
Ludic activities (curiosity, exploration) Drift Table (2004) supports non-task exploration. Al-Jazari's Floating Orchestra (1206): water-powered boat with programmable musicians. Promotes exploratory play via surprises, blurring real and mechanical in a “magic circle.”
Mind-body push-and-pull Brainball (2000) contests control for fun. Vaucanson's Digesting Duck (1739): eats, “digests” grain into pellets. Interactive feeding evokes ambiguity (is it alive?), with randomness like immersion in games.
Magic circle Draws users into enchanted zones, as in pervasive games. The Silver Swan (1773) by Cox and Merlin: preens, catches fish, swims with music. Creates a reflective zone through affectionate motions, tuning to cultural wonder.
Openness and ambiguity Memories reimagined with twists, like magical elements or alternate endings. Jaquet-Droz's The Writer (1774): a programmable boy that writes custom messages with fluid motions. Fosters curiosity through “hidden” programming, allowing users to explore endless story variations, much like branching narratives in games.
Curiosity and exploration Users explore story branches, akin to pervasive games. The Turk (1770) by Wolfgang von Kempelen: a chess-playing “automaton” (later revealed as a hoax with a hidden operator). Sparks wonder and investigation into its mechanisms, blending deception with playful challenge, encouraging repeated engagement.
Non-utilitarian fun Focuses on ludic activities, blurring play and life. Tipoo's Tiger (1790s): a life-sized automaton of a tiger mauling a European soldier, with sounds and movements. Delivers surprise and delight through dramatic, non-practical spectacle, evoking emotional responses like awe or humour in a historical context.
